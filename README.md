Portfolio Reflection: CS 250 Sprint Review and Retrospective
Artifact Overview
As part of the Computer Science program portfolio, this artifact captures the Sprint Review and Retrospective deliverable developed in CS 250. This deliverable synthesizes core Agile and Scrum methodologies applied throughout the course, highlighting the iterative planning, execution, and continuous improvement processes essential to modern software engineering.

Essential Questions & Reflections
1. How do I interpret user needs and implement them into a program? How does creating “user stories” help with this?
Interpreting user needs begins with active listening, stakeholder engagement, and bridging the gap between non-technical business requirements and technical specifications. Rather than guessing what an end-user wants, creating user stories—typically formatted as "As a [user persona], I want [feature] so that [benefit]"—forces developers to view software entirely from the user's perspective. User stories help break down large, ambiguous goals into manageable, value-driven increments. By defining clear acceptance criteria alongside each story, developers have concrete benchmarks for success, ensuring that the software built directly solves real user pain points and delivers measurable value.

2. How do I approach developing programs? What Agile processes do I hope to incorporate into my future development work?
My approach to software development centers on iterative problem-solving, modular architecture, and continuous testing rather than monolithic, waterfall-style delivery. Building programs in smaller, functional iterations allows for early validation and reduces the risk of massive rework later. Moving forward, I plan to actively incorporate key Agile processes into my development workflows, specifically:

Sprint Planning and Backlog Grooming: Breaking features into prioritized backlog items to maintain clear momentum and focus.

Daily Stand-ups: Keeping a disciplined rhythm to identify blockers early and align daily goals.

Sprint Reviews and Retrospectives: Regularly demoing functional code to gather feedback and introspect on team processes to continuously optimize how we work.

3. What does it mean to be a good team member in software development?
Being a strong team member in software engineering goes far beyond writing clean code; it requires transparent communication, accountability, and empathy. A good team member proactively communicates blockers during daily stand-ups rather than working in isolation, ensuring the entire team can adjust. It also means respecting diverse technical perspectives during code reviews, offering constructive feedback with kindness, and taking ownership of shared deliverables. Ultimately, software development is a collaborative team sport where mutual trust and psychological safety enable teams to solve complex problems faster and more effectively than any individual could alone.
